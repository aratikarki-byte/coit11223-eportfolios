## Introduction

**What is ADM?** Automated decision-making (ADM) is when a computer program makes a decision, or does something substantially and directly related to making a decision, with little or no human input. Governments use it for things like welfare payments, debt recovery and fraud detection.

**Why does it matter to me as an ICT professional?** ICT professionals build and test these systems, so we share responsibility for their impact on people. A biased or unexplained decision can cut someone off from income or services. The ACS Code of Professional Conduct asks us to put the public interest first, and ethical theories such as Kantianism and Act Utilitarianism help me judge whether a system treats people fairly.

**What I thought before this unit:** I had never really thought about whether it was fair for computers to make government decisions. I used to think computers made decisions based on data, so they would be more objective. However, I now realise that computer systems can be biased if the data they use is unfair. This made me understand that important government decisions should involve human judgment, transparency and accountability.

## Artefact 1: Automated Decision-Making (Safeguards and Transparency) Bill 2026 (Australia)

**Link:** https://www.katechaney.com.au/adm-bill-2026
Kate Chaney MP brought forth a bill on 7 September 2026 establishing Australia's first mandatory and legislated framework for ADM in government. The framework entails a notification requirement that requires informing individuals when an ADM system has made an automated decision concerning them, creation of a publicly accessible register of automated systems, requiring human intervention in cases of high-risk decisions, and granting an automatic right of review (Chaney 2026). This bill has been proposed as per the recommendations of the Robodebt Royal Commission that called for the establishment of a legislated framework.

The reason for selecting this topic is that it is evident that the regulatory regime of ADM has become an emerging issue in Australia currently. This bill is relevant in relation to the ACS Code of Conduct's principle of Primacy of the Public Interest and Kantianism since people have to be treated as individuals. This bill also changed my mind since previously, I trusted automated systems to be impartial.

## Artefact 2: Government use of automated AI decision-making to be curbed under new Australian rules (Australia)

**Link:** https://www.theguardian.com/australia-news/2026/jul/19/national-ai-plan-labor-anthony-albanese-andrew-charlton
The Guardian article (2026) indicates that the Australian Government is working on strict guidelines regarding the use of AI for automated decision-making at departments and agencies in line with the National AI plan. These guidelines are also expected to cover consumer protection, workplace health and safety, and privacy issues. This framework is a policy commitment and is not legally binding.

I selected this artifact to contrast the approach taken by the government in relation to the private member's bill presented in Artefact 1. This is relevant because it identifies who is responsible if automated systems make mistakes. This relates to the values of Honesty and Professionalism in the ACS Code as agencies have to be transparent in their decision-making process. This also relates to Act Utilitarianism as governments are evaluating the benefits of ADM against the damage it can do to people.

## Artefact 3: New OAIC guidance highlights expectations for government agencies using ADM (Australia)

**Link:** https://www.holdingredlich.com/new-oaic-guidance-highlights-expectations-for-government-agencies-using-automated-decision-making
The law firm article (Holding Redlich 2026) discusses new guidelines issued by the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner. As of 10 December 2026, agencies will have to revise their privacy policies so that they provide information on how they employ ADM in the case of a computer program making decisions or performing an action that is substantially and directly related to a decision. These guidelines cover both positive and negative decisions.

It was interesting for me because of the practical application of the idea of transparency as a legal requirement. As a prospective ICT professional, I may have to provide information on how a particular system comes to a decision so that it could be audited. It is related to the ACS Code principle of Honesty as well as Kantian universalisability – if all agencies were hiding how they were using their systems, people would completely lose trust.

## Artefact 4: Coded Injustice: Denmark's automated welfare system (Denmark)

**Link:** https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2024/11/denmark-ai-powered-welfare-system-fuels-mass-surveillance-and-risks-discriminating-against-marginalized-groups-report/
According to Amnesty International (2024), the welfare agencies in Denmark are using algorithmic anti-fraud systems which pose the danger of mass surveillance and discrimination against marginalised people. According to Fortune (2024), it is stated in the report that 90 per cent of those cases which were started as a result of one of these algorithms turned out to be non-fraudulent cases. Amnesty asked for far more careful use of people's information.

I chose it as my example of an overseas government policy because it is very similar to the situation described in my Question 1 case in which a model was prioritising certain people over others. This case could be analysed from the point of view of Act Utilitarianism since it is difficult to justify harm to many innocent people by small fraud prevention gains. It was also related to the ACS code of ethics – the Primacy of the Public Interest.

## Artefact 5: Uncovering discrimination in public fraud detection systems (Netherlands)

**Link:** https://media.ccc.de/v/dgwk2025-56344-uncovering-discrimination
As part of this speech in 2025, Willy Tadema, a specialist in artificial intelligence employed by the Dutch government, states that the algorithmic system developed for detecting fraud related to welfare, allowances and student loans had been discriminatory and was causing financial and psychological damage to people (Tadema 2025). The speaker analyses the reasons, the reasons for overlooking the problem, and how the governments should react to such events. It turns out that similar systems have been causing damage in Australia, the UK, Denmark and Sweden.

I selected this source since it provides the insider's point of view and draws a parallel between the Dutch child benefit scandal and the Robodebt case in Australia and shows the same errors being made. It relates to the value of Competence and Primacy of the Public Interest from the ACS Code since professionals should identify harm and raise the issue in time.

## Artefact 6: EU Artificial Intelligence Act, 2024 (European Union)

**Link:** https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj
EU Artificial Intelligence Act (European Parliament and Council of the European Union 2024) is a legally binding regulation of risk-based AI. It classifies AI used by public authorities to make decisions on access to essential public services as high-risk AI, and introduces regulations on transparency, human supervision and discrimination testing.

I have chosen this artefact to illustrate how other countries implement a binding legislation in this area in contrast to Australia which still creates a legislative framework (Artefacts 1 to 3). This is connected to the Social Contract Theory as citizens agree with the use of government power if it is fair and transparent. It is also relevant to ACS Code which puts a lot of emphasis on public interest.
